ACTIVITY 01

Order matters

Why you are doing this. The oldest way of handling text was to count the words and ignore the order they came in. That sounds harmless until you see what it does to a sentence like “good, not bad”. This activity exists so that when someone asks you why attention mattered, you can say what was actually broken before it — rather than just repeating that it was a breakthrough.

What to do. Five questions. Read each one, decide your answer before you click, then click the option you think is right. The explanation appears straight away. If you get one wrong, read why before moving on — that is the part that sticks.

Nothing to prepare · about five minutes
Score: 0 / 5

The point of this one

Bag-of-words is not a museum piece — spam filters, document routing and the keyword half of search still use it, and it is often the right answer. What matters is knowing exactly which questions it will get backwards, because those are the questions where the whole history of this field starts.

ACTIVITY 02

Attention in business writing

Why you are doing this. This is preparation for the first discussion question — what attention lets a model do that older methods could not. Most people answer it with “it focuses on the important words”, which is the word handed back rather than an explanation. The aim here is to give you something concrete to say instead, using the kind of sentences you will meet in a tender or an interview transcript.

What to do. Five questions. Answer each on instinct first, then read the explanation — they are written the way you could say them out loud in class. By the end you should be able to name two things older methods got wrong and one thing attention does instead.

Stuck? Open the attention viewer or the matrix in the labs tab and watch where a word sends its attention
Score: 0 / 5

How to answer Q1 out loud

Name both older failures — order thrown away, or order respected but forgotten. Say what attention does in one sentence: every word looks at every other word and weighs which matter, at any distance, in one step. Then give a document example, and close on the consequence: removing the sequential step made parallel training possible, which is why models could grow at all.

ACTIVITY 03

Encoder or decoder?

Why you are doing this. There are two families of these models. One reads and sorts: small, cheap, and able to run inside a client's own network. The other writes: large, and rented by the word. Clients constantly hire the expensive one to do the cheap one's job. Telling them apart is the most commercially useful thing in this session.

What to do. Five real tasks from an engagement. For each, decide whether you would reach for the reader or the writer, click your choice, and read why. Look for the pattern across the five rather than memorising them individually.

A rule to test as you go: if what you hand the client is a label, a number or a ranking it is the reader; if it is prose it is the writer
Score: 0 / 5

The pattern worth memorising

If the deliverable is a label, a number, a cluster or a ranking, look at the encoder family: small, cheap, runs inside your own network. If the deliverable is prose, it is the decoder family: large, rented, priced per token. And if the answer has to come from your own documents, it is usually both — one to find, one to write.

ACTIVITY 04

The three zones

Why you are doing this. This is preparation for the second discussion question — how the training cut-off should change the way you check facts. A model read an enormous amount, stopped on a date, and has never seen anything belonging to your client. Those are three different kinds of not-knowing, and it sounds equally sure about all three. The aim is that you learn to sort a claim before trying to check it, because the check is different for each kind.

What to do. Ten questions a client might ask. For each, decide which kind it is — something the model read, something that happened after it stopped reading or that changes often, or something that was never public at all — then click and read the explanation.

The third kind is the one people forget, and it is the one that causes trouble on a live engagement
Score: 0 / 10

What your misses tell you

Almost everyone handles the first two zones well and under-uses the third. If you marked a client-specific question as “verify”, ask yourself where you would go to verify it — there is no source, because the information was never published. The action there is not checking, it is attaching.

ACTIVITY 05

Build the stack

Why you are doing this. A model straight out of training does not answer questions at all — it simply carries on your sentence. Everything that makes it feel like a helpful assistant was added afterwards, layer by layer, by people. Knowing which layer does what means that when a vendor pitches you, you can work out what they are really selling and what it will not fix.

What to do. First open the stack lab in the labs tab. Switch every layer off and read what comes back, then add them one at a time and watch the answer change. Come back here and answer the five questions.

These cannot be answered from memory — they are written so that you have to have played with the lab first
Score: 0 / 5

Why this is the most commercial thing in the session

The two layers you control — your documents and your brief — cost the least and change the output the most. The layer most often sold to you, house-style tuning, buys manner rather than knowledge. Being able to say which layer a proposal is actually selling is worth more in a client meeting than being able to explain self-attention.

ACTIVITY 06

Client objections

Why you are doing this. Knowing how something works and being able to explain it to a sceptical client are different skills, and only the second one gets you paid. These are six things clients genuinely say. Each has an answer that is honest without being either dismissive or oversold, and finding that middle ground is the skill being practised.

What to do. Read the objection on each card. Say your answer out loud, in your own words, before you open the card — actually out loud, because that is where you find out whether you know it. Then open the card and see what you left out.

No scores here · the measure is whether your answer would have satisfied the person who asked
ACTIVITY 07

Self-check

score 0 / 10
Why you are doing this. A quick check on whether the whole story stuck, from counting words in the 1950s through to the training cut-off. It covers every module in the notes, so it also shows you which part is worth going back and re-reading.

What to do. Ten questions, straight through, without looking anything up. Answer on instinct. The explanation appears the moment you choose, so a wrong answer here is worth more than a right one — it tells you exactly where the gap is.

If you miss two from the same module, that is the module to revisit
Debrief — five questions for yourself
  • Can you tell the bag-of-words story in three sentences? What it did, what it threw away, and why that mattered. If you can, the rest of the arc has somewhere to attach to.
  • Which zone did you misjudge in activity 04? Nearly everyone under-uses the third one, and it is the one that shows up in a client deliverable.
  • Could you answer Q1 without using the word “attention” twice? Explaining a term by repeating it is the most common weak answer in the room.
  • In the stack lab, which layer surprised you? Most people are startled that the raw model does not answer questions at all until it is taught to.
  • One habit to take away. Anything with a date or a decimal point gets sourced; anything about the client gets attached. Pick one and use it this week.

LAB 01 · The story on one lineClick each stop and say the one-sentence version out loud before reading it.
The whole story on one line click a stop
The whole session in five sentences
  • Count the words. Turn a sentence into a row of numbers by counting how often each word appears. Simple, and it loses the word order completely.
  • Give each word its own numbers. Words that appear beside the same other words get similar numbers. Now the machine can tell that "client" and "customer" are related.
  • Read the sentence in order. Better, but the model has to squeeze everything into one small summary, so it forgets the start of long text.
  • Let every word look at every other word. This is attention. Nothing gets forgotten, however far apart the words are.
  • Make it enormous. Once step four removed the need to read in order, training could be split across thousands of machines — so the models grew, and started drafting memos.

Four things to notice
  • No single number means anything. The third number is not "formality" and the fifth is not "money". Ask what one of them stands for and there is no answer — the meaning lives in the pattern of all of them together.
  • Similar words have similar rows. Compare "invoice" with "payment" and the bars rise and fall together. Compare either with "monsoon" and they go their own way.
  • Nobody typed these in. They started as random numbers and got nudged, a fraction at a time, across billions of sentences, until words that keep the same company ended up with matching rows.
  • Real ones are much longer. Eight numbers here so you can see them. A real embedding has several hundred, which is why it can hold far finer distinctions than a picture on a page can show.
LAB 04 · The meaning mapPick a word and predict its three nearest neighbours before you click.
Lab · the meaning map click a word

A real embedding space has hundreds of dimensions, which nobody can draw. This is a flattened version with the same property: distance means similarity. Click any word to see its nearest neighbours and what the model has effectively learned about it.

How a model learns this, step by step
  • Give every word a random list of numbers. Fifty or so numbers each, meaning nothing at all to begin with. This is the starting point, and it is deliberately random.
  • Take two words from a real sentence. Ask the model a yes-or-no question: are these two likely to be neighbours?
  • Check the answer against reality. The sentence already tells you whether they were neighbours or not. So you know immediately whether the guess was right.
  • Nudge the numbers. If two words really do appear together, shift their number-lists slightly closer. If they do not, shift them apart. One tiny nudge per pair.
  • Repeat a few billion times. Words that keep the same company drift together; words that never co-occur drift apart. Nobody ever told it that "invoice" and "payment" are related — that fell out of the counting.

LAB 07 · The attention matrixRead one row: that is a single word spending its 100% of attention. Find the bright square.
Lab · the whole picture at once every word against every word

The viewer above shows one word looking around. This shows all of them at the same time. Read a row: that is one word, and how it splits its attention across the sentence. Brighter means more attention. Click any row label to follow it, or click a single square to read it out in words.

How to read this grid, step by step
  • Every row is one word doing the looking. The row labelled "it" shows where the word "it" sends its attention.
  • Every column is one word being looked at. A bright column means lots of other words care about that word.
  • Each row adds up to 100%. A word has a fixed amount of attention to spend, so giving more to one word means giving less to another. That is why the bright squares are so few.
  • The diagonal is a word looking at itself. Usually a little bright — a word's own meaning matters to its own meaning — but rarely the brightest thing in the row.
  • The one bright square off the diagonal is the interesting one. In row "it", it sits under "animal". That single square is the model resolving what "it" refers to, and it is the whole reason this mechanism exists.
  • Real models run several of these grids at once. One might track grammar, another track what refers to what, another track verbs and their objects. The results get combined. Same picture, several times over.

ACTIVITY 01

Score the prompt

Why you are doing this. Anyone can tell a terrible prompt from an excellent one. The skill worth having is telling a 2 from a 3 — because that is the judgement that tells you what to fix. This activity builds that calibration before you start scoring your own work, where you will be far too generous.

What to do. Five questions about real prompts and their scores. Decide your answer before you click. Where a score is given, work out for yourself why it is not one point higher.

Five questions · about five minutes · open the evaluator in the labs tab if you want the dimension definitions beside you
Score: 0 / 5

The habit this builds

Scoring before sending turns iteration from rewording-and-hoping into targeted repair. When the output disappoints, you will be able to say “constraints were a 1 and output definition a 2” rather than “that wasn't great, let me try again” — and the fix takes one revision instead of five.

ACTIVITY 02

Pick the framework

Why you are doing this. Six frameworks sounds like five too many. In practice you need RTF and CREATE fluently, and you need to recognise when a task has the shape that one of the other four was built for — a process that will drift, brand copy that will go generic, training material pitched at the wrong level, an advisory ask with no ask in it.

What to do. Five real situations. For each, decide which framework fits before you click, and pay attention to the two questions where the obvious answer is wrong.

The framework explorer in the labs tab has a full worked prompt for each of the four specialised ones
Score: 0 / 5

What to actually remember

Do not memorise six frameworks. Learn RTF and CREATE properly and treat the rest as reminders of what a given output type usually needs: RISEN reminds you to bound the scope, COSTAR that style and tone are different decisions, TRACE that audience changes everything, PARA that a consulting prompt needs an explicit ask.

ACTIVITY 03

Diagnose the failure

Why you are doing this. Five symptoms you will meet this week, each with a specific cause. Learning to read the symptom means your next prompt fixes the actual problem instead of adding words at random and hoping.

What to do. Five disappointing outputs. For each, decide which dimension failed and what the one-line fix is — before you click.

Every one of these has a fix that takes under thirty seconds to write
Score: 0 / 5

The diagnostic table, short version

Generic output → you attached nothing. Inconsistent structure → output definition. Recommends the impossible → no exclusions. Tone nearly right but not quite → stop describing, show two examples. Confident and unchallengeable → name the reasoning steps and ask what would reverse the answer.

ACTIVITY 04 · IN CLAUDE

Write a CREATE prompt for Sunita Kapoor

Why you are doing this. Recognising a good prompt and writing one are different skills. This is the writing one — with the scenario handed to you, so all your effort goes into the prompt rather than into inventing a situation. About twenty minutes in your own Claude Pro subscription.
The scenario MedCore Health · healthcare · 3,200 employees · 6 hospitals, 40 clinics

Sunita Kapoor is MedCore's Chief Medical Officer. Nurse turnover in the ICU units hit 28% in 2024 — fourteen points above the industry benchmark. She has asked you to help her build the retention strategy she will take to the board.

What the exit interviews said
Burnout — 41% · Scheduling — 33% · Pay gap versus competitors — 26%
A competitor hospital hired 60 ICU nurses away during the year.

What she cannot do
Nothing over $2M of capital. No hiring freezes. It must be actionable within 6 months.

What she needs back
A prioritised action plan she can present: three actions in the next 30 days, three in 90 days, and one structural change at 180 days.
What to do — four steps.

1 · Type the lazy version first. In Claude, ask for it the way you would have before this session — something like “help me build a nurse retention strategy”. Run it. Keep the answer. This is your control, and skipping it is the one thing that ruins the exercise.

2 · Now write the CREATE version. Six lines, one per letter, using only the facts in the box above. Open the CREATE builder in the labs tab if you want the fields laid out for you — it assembles the prompt and gives you a copy button.

C — who should Claude be? · R — the exact task · E — the exit data and the competitor raid · A — the $2M ceiling, no hiring freezes, six months · T — the 30/90/180 plan shape · E — ask it to rate confidence and flag policy assumptions

3 · Run it in a fresh conversation. Fresh matters — in the same thread it has already seen your first answer and you are no longer comparing prompts.

4 · Compare, then check yourself. Put the two answers side by side and count the sentences you would rewrite in each. Then open the model answer below and see what you left out.
C: You are MedCore's Chief People Officer with 20 years of hospital workforce management experience.
R: Develop a nurse retention strategy for ICU units where turnover hit 28% in 2024 — 14pts above benchmark.
E: Exit data: burnout 41%, scheduling 33%, pay gap 26%. Competitor hired 60 ICU nurses away.
A: Actionable within 6 months. No options over $2M capital. No hiring freezes.
T: Prioritized action plan: 3 actions in 30 days, 3 in 90 days, 1 structural change at 180 days.
E: Rate confidence per recommendation. Flag policy assumptions.

Notice how short it is. Six lines, no clever phrasing, no persuasion — just six decisions made by the writer instead of the model. If yours is longer than this and scored lower, the extra words were not information.

What usually happens

Three things, almost every time. The CREATE version needs roughly a third as much editing. The A line quietly deletes a whole section the lazy version produced — pay rises above the cap, or a recruitment freeze — that you would otherwise have removed by hand. And the confidence ratings from the final E point straight at the weakest recommendation, which you would otherwise have found by reading everything twice.

Bring both prompts and both answers to class, plus your rewrite count. If the briefed version came out worse, bring that — it happens when over-specifying narrows a task that wanted range, and it is a more interesting five minutes than another success story.

ACTIVITY 05 · IN CLAUDE

Write in Lena Wolff's voice

Why you are doing this. Few-shot is the technique everyone reads about and nobody tries, and it produces the most startling before-and-after in the session. It teaches one thing no amount of reading can: you cannot write instructions that produce a good sentence. About fifteen minutes in Claude Pro.
The scenario Verdant Retail · sustainable fashion · 220 stores · $1.2B revenue

Lena Wolff is Verdant's Brand Director. She wants Claude to write product descriptions in her exact voice — warm, direct, sustainability-focused, and completely free of buzzwords. Here are two descriptions she wrote herself.

Organic cotton tee — "Made from 100% GOTS-certified organic cotton. Soft enough to sleep in, structured enough for Mondays. No dyes you can't pronounce. A tee built to last 10 years, not 10 washes."

Recycled wool coat — "This coat began as six wool sweaters. We unraveled them, re-spun the fiber, and wove something better. The weight of something that has already lived a life. Designed for another 20 years."

The new product — Unlined linen blazer. Belgian linen, no synthetic blends, wrinkle-resistant.
What to do — four steps.

1 · Try describing the voice. In Claude, write your best paragraph describing Lena's style — warm, short sentences, whatever you notice — and ask for the linen blazer description. Read it. It will be competent, plausible, and not her voice.

2 · Now show instead of telling. New conversation. Paste both of her descriptions as examples, add her rules, and ask for the blazer. The few-shot lab in the labs tab has advice on choosing examples if you want it.

Her rules: short sentences · one tactile detail · one sourcing fact · under 60 words · and no buzzwords — ban “eco-friendly”, “sustainable”, “planet”, “green”

3 · Put the two answers side by side and mark the specific sentences where the second is closer. It usually is not subtle.

4 · Then run the experiment that makes the point. Same prompt, but delete the banned-word list. Watch how quickly it drifts back to “eco-friendly” and “sustainable” — the exact two words a sustainability brand has to avoid.
Write Verdant product descriptions matching my exact voice.

Example 1 — Organic cotton tee:
"Made from 100% GOTS-certified organic cotton. Soft enough to sleep in, structured enough for Mondays. No dyes you can't pronounce. A tee built to last 10 years, not 10 washes."

Example 2 — Recycled wool coat:
"This coat began as six wool sweaters. We unraveled them, re-spun the fiber, and wove something better. The weight of something that has already lived a life. Designed for another 20 years."

Rules: Short sentences. One tactile detail. One sourcing fact. No buzzwords (eco-friendly, sustainable, planet, green). Under 60 words.

Now write for: Unlined linen blazer, Belgian linen, no synthetic blends, wrinkle-resistant.

Two examples, five rules, one task. The banned-word list is doing more work than any description of tone could — and notice that the examples were chosen to be different from each other: one about fabric, one about a whole origin story.

Where this goes in real consulting work

The same mechanism handles four jobs you will do repeatedly: cloning your firm's note format from two approved past notes, coding thirty interview transcripts consistently after coding five by hand, matching a client's brand voice, and classifying anything into your own categories. In each case, do a small sample by hand and let the examples carry the specification.

And when you find yourself pasting the same examples a third time, stop — that is the signal to package them as a project instruction or a saved skill. It is also the moment you stop being a fluent user and start building an asset, which is where Module 2 of this course picks up.

ACTIVITY 06

Client objections

Why you are doing this. Six things clients and colleagues genuinely say about prompt engineering — several of them fair. Being able to answer without either dismissing the scepticism or overselling the discipline is what makes you useful in the meeting rather than just enthusiastic.

What to do. Read each objection. Say your answer out loud, in your own words, before opening the card — actually out loud, because that is where you find out whether you know it. Then open it and see what you left out.

No scores · the measure is whether your answer would have satisfied the person who asked

Debrief — and the post-session reflection

The course asks you to take a weak prompt and iterate it through at least three versions, submitting all of them with notes on what changed and why the output improved. Activity 04 is that exercise with one extra step: score each version out of 20 in the evaluator, so your notes carry numbers rather than adjectives.

  • Start genuinely weak. Use a real prompt from your own history. A staged weak version produces a staged improvement and teaches nobody anything, least of all you.
  • Change one dimension per version. If version two adds context, constraints and a format all at once, you have learned that adding things helps — which you knew. Change one and you learn what each is worth.
  • Put the scores in your notes. "The output improved" becomes "context went from 1 to 3 and the invented figures disappeared". One of those sentences is evidence.
  • Include something that did not work. Most real iteration contains a change that made no difference or made things worse. Reporting it is what distinguishes a lab notebook from a marketing document.
  • Then answer the discussion question honestly. Which technique most improved your results, and why — mechanically, not "it just worked better". For most people in a room like this it will be output format or role, and saying so is a finding rather than a failure of imagination.

How to use this on your own work
  • Score before you send, not after. The whole point is catching a missing dimension while it costs thirty seconds rather than after three rounds of disappointing output.
  • Calibrate on the worked examples first. Score the three MedCore prompts below and compare with the published scores. Telling a 2 from a 3 is the hard part, and it comes from practice rather than from reading the definitions.
  • Fix the lowest dimension first. Not the one you find most interesting. A prompt scoring 4,4,0,4,4 is failing on constraints, and adding more context will not help it.
  • Do not chase 20 out of 20. A 19 usually means the last point needed data you genuinely do not have — which is a fact about your engagement, not a flaw in your prompt. Flag the gap in the prompt instead.

From a bag of words
to something that writes your memo.

One question runs through this whole session: how do you turn words into numbers a computer can work with, without losing what they mean? Every method here is a better answer than the one before it. We start by counting words in a bag and end with a machine that drafts your memo. Two discussion questions sit at the end.

The whole story on one line click a stop
The whole session in five sentences
  • Count the words. Turn a sentence into a row of numbers by counting how often each word appears. Simple, and it loses the word order completely.
  • Give each word its own numbers. Words that appear beside the same other words get similar numbers. Now the machine can tell that "client" and "customer" are related.
  • Read the sentence in order. Better, but the model has to squeeze everything into one small summary, so it forgets the start of long text.
  • Let every word look at every other word. This is attention. Nothing gets forgotten, however far apart the words are.
  • Make it enormous. Once step four removed the need to read in order, training could be split across thousands of machines — so the models grew, and started drafting memos.
01
Where it starts

Counting words in a bag

A computer cannot read. It can only do sums. So the first problem was never intelligence — it was arithmetic: how do you turn a sentence into numbers? The first real answer arrived in the 1950s and it is as simple as it sounds.

The picture to hold on toTip the sentence into a shopping bag. Shake it. Count what fell out. That is bag-of-words — and once you have counts, you have numbers, and once you have numbers a computer can finally do something with language.
Lab · the bag-of-words machine edit the sentences

Two sentences go in. The machine splits them into tokens, collects every distinct word into a shared vocabulary, and counts how often each one appears in each sentence. Those counts are the numbers — the vector — that stand in for the sentence from then on.

How it works, step by step
  • Split the sentence into words. "The store visit was good" becomes five separate pieces. These pieces are called tokens. Splitting on spaces is the usual method — though it fails for languages like Mandarin that do not put spaces between words.
  • Make one shared list of every word used. Both sentences pool their words into a single list, with no duplicates. This list is the vocabulary, and every word in it becomes a column.
  • Count. For each sentence, count how many times each word in the vocabulary appears. Most counts are zero.
  • Keep the counts and throw the sentence away. From here on the sentence is that row of numbers. Everything downstream — sorting, searching, classifying — works from the counts alone.
  • Notice what just vanished. Word order. "Good, not bad" and "bad, not good" contain exactly the same words, so they produce exactly the same numbers. The machine now believes two opposite sentences are identical.
What it got right
  • It made language computable. Once a document is a row of numbers you can sort it, cluster it, search it and feed it to a statistical model. Everything that follows depends on that one move.
  • It is fast and transparent. You can read the vector and see exactly why a document was classified the way it was — a property most of what came later quietly lost.
  • It still works. Spam filters, document routing, and the keyword half of modern search engines all still count words. When a partner asks whether you need a large language model to tag five thousand support tickets, the honest answer is often no.
What it gets wrong
  • Order is thrown away. "Good, not bad" and "bad, not good" produce identical numbers. Try it in the lab above. For a consultant reading customer verbatims, that is not a rounding error — it is the opposite conclusion.
  • Meaning is invisible. "Client", "customer" and "buyer" are three unrelated columns. Nothing in the numbers knows they are near-synonyms.
  • The vocabulary explodes. A real corpus has tens of thousands of distinct words, so almost every number in every vector is a zero. Vast, empty and dumb.
The problem this leaves behind. Bag-of-words tells you which words are present. It has no way of knowing that two different words mean nearly the same thing, or that the same word means two different things. Every advance for the next fifty years is an attempt to fix exactly that.
02
2013 · word2vec

Meaning, written down as numbers

The fix came in 2013, and it rests on one simple observation: you can tell what a word means by the company it keeps. "Invoice" turns up near "payment". "Payment" turns up near "terms". Words that keep the same company probably mean related things.

The picture to hold on toThink of a seating plan at a large wedding. Nobody wrote down who is related to whom — but if you watch who sits with whom over many weddings, the families become obvious. Embeddings are that seating plan, worked out by watching billions of sentences.
An embedding is that list of numbers — usually a few hundred of them — standing for one word, one sentence or one whole document. Nobody chooses what the numbers mean. They come out of the training process, and their only job is to place similar things near each other in a space with far more than three dimensions.

Four things to notice
  • No single number means anything. The third number is not "formality" and the fifth is not "money". Ask what one of them stands for and there is no answer — the meaning lives in the pattern of all of them together.
  • Similar words have similar rows. Compare "invoice" with "payment" and the bars rise and fall together. Compare either with "monsoon" and they go their own way.
  • Nobody typed these in. They started as random numbers and got nudged, a fraction at a time, across billions of sentences, until words that keep the same company ended up with matching rows.
  • Real ones are much longer. Eight numbers here so you can see them. A real embedding has several hundred, which is why it can hold far finer distinctions than a picture on a page can show.
Lab · the meaning map click a word

A real embedding space has hundreds of dimensions, which nobody can draw. This is a flattened version with the same property: distance means similarity. Click any word to see its nearest neighbours and what the model has effectively learned about it.

How a model learns this, step by step
  • Give every word a random list of numbers. Fifty or so numbers each, meaning nothing at all to begin with. This is the starting point, and it is deliberately random.
  • Take two words from a real sentence. Ask the model a yes-or-no question: are these two likely to be neighbours?
  • Check the answer against reality. The sentence already tells you whether they were neighbours or not. So you know immediately whether the guess was right.
  • Nudge the numbers. If two words really do appear together, shift their number-lists slightly closer. If they do not, shift them apart. One tiny nudge per pair.
  • Repeat a few billion times. Words that keep the same company drift together; words that never co-occur drift apart. Nobody ever told it that "invoice" and "payment" are related — that fell out of the counting.
Why this mattered to the work you will do
  • Search stopped needing the exact word. Ask for "supplier payment delays" and a document saying "vendor invoice arrears" now comes back. Every semantic search and retrieval system in a consulting firm rests on this.
  • Clustering became meaningful. Four hundred open-ended survey answers group themselves by what respondents meant, not by which words they happened to use.
  • Classification got cheap. Turn a document into an embedding, and a simple statistical model on top can sort it — no rules, no keyword lists to maintain.
  • Levels of abstraction. Embeddings exist for words, sentences, whole documents, and for images and audio too. Bag-of-words was always a document-level embedding; word2vec gave us word-level ones.
The flaw it could not fix

A word2vec embedding is static. The word "bank" gets one list of numbers, downloaded once, used everywhere. But look at these two sentences:

We should approach the bank about refinancing.
The plant sits on the bank of the Sabarmati.

One word, two unrelated meanings, one frozen set of numbers that has to serve both. The same problem hits every word that matters in business writing: "interest", "capital", "margin", "security", "returns", "charge". The meaning is decided by the sentence around the word — and word2vec never looks at the sentence.

Fixing this is what the next decade of work was about, and it is the direct ancestor of the thing you type into today.

03
Recurrent networks

Reading in order, and forgetting

If meaning depends on the sentence, the machine has to actually read the sentence. So the next design did: one word at a time, left to right, keeping a running summary in its head as it went. Two of these working together could translate — one reads the English, one writes the Dutch.

The picture to hold on toChinese whispers. A message is passed down a line of people, each one hearing it, holding it, and passing it on. With five people it arrives fine. With fifty, the beginning is long gone — and nobody in the line knows anything has been lost.
Lab · watch the bottleneck bite drag the slider

One fixed-size summary has to carry the whole input. Lengthen the input and see what survives.

Why long text broke it, step by step
  • The reader takes one word at a time. After each word it updates a single running summary — one fixed-size list of numbers, no bigger for a long sentence than a short one.
  • Each new word writes over the summary. The summary has to make room, so the oldest information gets faded to fit the newest.
  • At the end, that one summary is all the writer gets. Everything the input meant has to pass through it. Three words fit comfortably. Forty do not.
  • The writer produces one word at a time too. Each word it writes is fed back in before the next is chosen — which is still exactly how your tools work today.
  • Nothing warns you. The output is fluent either way. It simply gets quietly worse as the input gets longer, which is the hardest kind of failure to notice.
And a second problem, which turned out to matter more. Because word two cannot start until word one is finished, none of this can be split across machines. That single constraint is what capped how big these models could ever get.
04
Discussion question 1

Attention: what it lets a model do that nothing before could

You are at a noisy party. Someone says: "The animal didn't cross the road because it was too tired." You know instantly that "it" means the animal. You did not re-read the sentence from the start — you just looked back at the right word. That is the whole idea, and in 2014 someone worked out how to give it to a machine.

The picture to hold on toLooking something up in a library. You have a topic in mind. You scan the labels on the spines. The ones that match get most of your attention, the rest get almost none — and what you walk away with is a blend of the few books that actually matched.
Attention lets every word in a sentence look at every other word and decide which ones matter to it — directly, at any distance, without having to remember its way back there.
Lab · look at what the model looks at pick a sentence, click a word
How attention works, step by step
  • Every word plays three parts. What it is looking for, a label advertising what it offers, and the information it hands over if chosen. Exactly like a library search: your topic, the labels on the spines, and the contents inside. (Their technical names are query, key and value — you can safely forget those.)
  • Compare. The word doing the looking — say "it" — checks itself against every other word's label and gets a match score for each.
  • Turn the scores into percentages. The scores become a split of attention that adds up to 100%. Perhaps 42% on "animal", 18% on "tired", 3% on "the".
  • Blend. The word takes a mixture of the other words' information, weighted by those percentages. Because most of the weight landed on "animal", the word "it" quietly picks up the meaning of "animal".
  • Do this for every word, all at once. Not just for "it" — every word in the sentence does the same lookup simultaneously. That is the step that older designs could never take.
The business-writing example to use in the room

A tender document, page 4:

"Late delivery shall attract a penalty of 0.5% of contract value per week, capped at 10%."

Page 31, twenty-seven pages later:

"The aforementioned penalties shall not apply where delay is attributable to the Purchaser."

To answer "what is our worst-case penalty exposure?", something has to connect those two sentences. A bag-of-words model cannot — it has no order at all. A sequential model reading page by page has long since forgotten page 4 by the time it reaches page 31.

Attention just reaches back. The words "the aforementioned penalties" look across the entire document, find the clause on page 4, and bind to it — at a distance of twelve thousand words, in one step, at no extra cost. That is the capability. Everything you find useful about these tools follows from it.

Three more places you will see it working
  • Pronouns and referents in interview notes. "She said the plant had raised it twice already" — attention is what binds "it" to the issue mentioned four sentences earlier, which is how thirty transcripts become a coherent theme table.
  • Tone carried across a document. Ask for a rewrite "for a sceptical chief financial officer" and every sentence attends back to that instruction, four pages later, without you repeating it.
  • Contradiction spotting. "Where do these two reports disagree?" is a question about relationships between distant passages. Attention is the mechanism that makes it answerable at all.
Say it this way in an exam or an interview. Older approaches either ignored word order entirely, or read in order and forgot. Attention lets the model weigh every word against every other word at once, so meaning that depends on something far away in the text finally survives.
Lab · the whole picture at once every word against every word

The viewer above shows one word looking around. This shows all of them at the same time. Read a row: that is one word, and how it splits its attention across the sentence. Brighter means more attention. Click any row label to follow it, or click a single square to read it out in words.

How to read this grid, step by step
  • Every row is one word doing the looking. The row labelled "it" shows where the word "it" sends its attention.
  • Every column is one word being looked at. A bright column means lots of other words care about that word.
  • Each row adds up to 100%. A word has a fixed amount of attention to spend, so giving more to one word means giving less to another. That is why the bright squares are so few.
  • The diagonal is a word looking at itself. Usually a little bright — a word's own meaning matters to its own meaning — but rarely the brightest thing in the row.
  • The one bright square off the diagonal is the interesting one. In row "it", it sits under "animal". That single square is the model resolving what "it" refers to, and it is the whole reason this mechanism exists.
  • Real models run several of these grids at once. One might track grammar, another track what refers to what, another track verbs and their objects. The results get combined. Same picture, several times over.
05
2017 · Attention Is All You Need

The Transformer, drawn out

For three years attention was a helper bolted onto the read-one-word-at-a-time design. In 2017 a paper asked the obvious question: if attention is doing the real work, why keep the reading-in-order part at all? They removed it. What was left is called the Transformer, and it is the T in GPT.

The picture to hold on toA factory line with two halves. On the left, a team of readers takes the input apart and works out what it means. On the right, a team of writers builds the output one word at a time, glancing back at the readers' notes whenever they need to. Each team has several identical stations stacked one above the other, and the work passes upward through them.
Step through the architecture twelve steps · press next, or play all
What is being traced. One short sentence — "I love llamas" — going in as English and coming out as Dutch. The diagram above, lit up one piece at a time, with a close-up of each step on the right.

What the machine is holding at this point
Lab · inside the block click a part
What one block does, step by step
  • Look up each word's numbers. Every token is swapped for its list of numbers — the grandchild of the 2013 idea, except these are learned inside the model rather than downloaded separately.
  • Add the position. Since every word is now handled at the same time, the model would otherwise have no idea which came first. So a marker for "I am word number seven" gets added to each one.
  • Run attention. Every word looks at every other word and blends in what it found. Several of these run side by side, each learning to spot a different kind of link — one tracks grammar, another tracks what "it" refers to.
  • Think about what was found. A small network digests the result. Attention decides what to look at; this step decides what to make of it.
  • Do it again, dozens of times. Block after block. Early blocks catch surface patterns, later ones handle meaning and structure. Nobody assigned those jobs — they emerged.
  • When writing, cover the future. A model learning to predict the next word must not be allowed to peek at it, so attention to anything ahead is switched off. That one rule is why text is generated strictly left to right.

2018 · the fork in the road

Two branches: BERT and GPT

The original design had two halves, because translation needs both: one to read the English, one to write the Dutch. In 2018 two teams each threw one half away — and those two halves are still the two families you will meet today.

The picture to hold on toA librarian and a writer. The librarian reads everything and can tell you which shelf a thing belongs on, what it resembles, and where to find more like it — but never writes you a page. The writer produces the page, but has to be handed the material first. Different jobs, different budgets, and clients regularly hire the writer to do the librarian's work.

Note the first line of GPT's history: it was trained on 7,000 books plus a large crawl of the web, and it had 117 million parameters. That was 2018, and it was considered big.

The one you actually use: a stack of writers, and nothing else

Take the picture from the last module and delete the entire left-hand side. No readers, no handover, no middle step. Just writer stations, stacked, each one looking backwards only. That is GPT — and it is the shape of every tool you have typed into.

Watch one word get produced, step by step
  • Everything so far goes in at the bottom. Your instructions, any document you pasted, the conversation, and whatever it has written of this answer already. All of it, every time.
  • Each word becomes numbers, plus a note of its position. Without the position note the model would not know which word came first, because it handles them all at once.
  • The stack of stations runs. At each station every word looks back at the earlier words and blends in what matters, then thinks it over. Dozens of stations, one after another.
  • At the top, every possible next word gets a score. Roughly a hundred thousand candidates, turned into percentages that add up to one hundred.
  • One is picked. Usually the top one; at higher temperature, sometimes a runner-up. This is the only place chance enters.
  • That word is added to the input, and the whole trip runs again. A four-hundred-word answer means roughly five hundred and fifty complete trips through the stack.
What this explains about the tool on your deskIt answers word by word because it literally produces one word per trip. It cannot go back and fix an earlier sentence, because that sentence is now part of its input. A long reply costs more and takes longer in direct proportion to its length. And it re-reads your entire conversation on every single turn — which is why a long chat over a big document quietly gets expensive.
Lab · which family does this task need? pick a task
How to tell which one you need, step by step
  • Ask what you are handing the client. A label, a number, a group or a ranking? That is the librarian — an encoder model. A page of prose? That is the writer — a decoder model.
  • Ask how many times you will do it. Forty thousand tickets is librarian territory: small model, your own servers, pennies. Nine transcripts into one note is writer territory.
  • Ask where the answer has to come from. If it must come from your own documents, you almost always need both — the librarian finds the right pages, the writer turns them into an answer. That two-step is what "retrieval" actually means.
  • Then check the data can leave. Encoder models are small enough to run inside your client's network. That single fact settles a lot of procurement conversations before they start.
Why a consultant should care about a distinction this technical. Because the answer changes the budget. "Tag every one of our 40,000 support tickets by issue type" sounds like a job for the tool everyone is talking about, and it is not — a small encoder model does it faster, cheaper, on your own servers, with no data leaving the building. Knowing which branch a problem belongs to is worth more in a scoping meeting than knowing how attention works.

07
Scale

Why "large" is in the name

Almost nothing about the design changed after 2017. What changed was size. More dials, more text, more computers — and on the way up, abilities nobody asked for started appearing. A small model finishes your sentence. A large one drafts your memo. Neither was taught to do anything except guess the next word.

The picture to hold on toParameters are dials. Billions of them, each set automatically during training, none of them labelled. More dials means more room to store patterns — grammar, genre, facts, the shape of an argument. It also means you can never open the machine and turn one dial to fix a wrong fact, because no single dial holds it.
Lab · turn the dial drag through six years of growth
Three things to say about scale without getting caught out
  • The early numbers are public; the recent ones are not. GPT-1 at 117 million and GPT-2 at 1.5 billion are documented. Today's frontier labs stopped publishing. If you see a figure quoted in the trillions, say "estimated" or say nothing.
  • Bigger is not automatically the right buy. Large models cost more per question and answer more slowly. For sorting, tagging and extracting, a small model often matches them. The big one earns its price on long documents and hard reasoning.
  • Scale did not fix invention. A larger model is wrong less often and more convincingly. Everything you learned last session about checking still applies, at every size.
What a parameter actually is

A dial. Billions of them, set during training, each one a number that shifts how strongly one part of the network responds to another. Nobody assigns meanings to them and nobody can read them back out. "More parameters" means more capacity to store patterns — grammar, genre, world knowledge, the shape of an argument — in a compressed and unreadable form.

This is also why you cannot open a model up and correct a fact. The fact is not stored anywhere you can point to. It is smeared across a great many dials.

Three honest cautions on scale
  • Published numbers stopped being published. GPT-1 at 117 million and GPT-2 at 1.5 billion are documented. For the recent frontier models the labs no longer disclose parameter counts — figures you see quoted in the trillions are estimates, not facts. Say "we do not know" rather than repeat a number you cannot source.
  • Bigger is not always the right buy. Larger costs more per query, responds more slowly, and for extraction or classification work a small model often matches it. The frontier model earns its price on reasoning and long documents, not on tagging tickets.
  • Scale did not fix invention. A bigger model is wrong less often and more convincingly. The verification habit from Session 01 does not get retired at any size.
08
The part vendors sell you

From a raw model to something a business can use

A model fresh out of training is not a product. It will happily carry on your sentence, but nobody has told it that a question wants an answer. It has no access to your files and no memory. Everything between that raw model and the helpful assistant on your screen was added afterwards, on purpose, by people.

The picture to hold on toAn engine is not a car. The engine is the expensive part and somebody else already built it. What turns it into something you can drive — the steering, the brakes, the fuel line to your own tank — is what you are actually buying when a vendor pitches you.
Lab · build the stack switch layers on and off

Start with the raw model and add the layers one at a time. Watch what the same request produces at each stage.

What each layer adds, step by step
  • Raw model. Guesses the next word. Ask it to summarise something and it will continue the phrase "summarise this" rather than doing it.
  • Instruction tuning. Trained on examples of instructions and good answers. Now it answers instead of continuing. Done once, by the lab.
  • Human preference training. People rank alternative answers and the model is nudged towards the preferred ones. This is where the politeness, the hedging and the refusals come from.
  • House-style tuning. Extra training on your firm's documents so output arrives in your structure and voice. Buys manner, not knowledge — and this is the layer most often over-sold.
  • Tools. Web search for recent facts, your own document store for private ones, a calculator so the arithmetic is actually calculated. This is where most real value gets added.
  • Your prompt. The brief you write, every single time. Costs nothing, changes the output the most, and is the only layer you fully control.

The corpus-tuning row is the one that gets over-sold — it buys manner, not knowledge, which is exactly the trap the ladder in Session 01 was built to avoid.

Discussion question 2

It stopped reading on a particular day

All of that happened before you opened the app. Training ended on a particular date and stopped. From that moment the model's picture of the world is a photograph, not a window.

The picture to hold on toSomeone brilliant who read every newspaper up to a certain date, then went into a room with no windows. Ask about anything before that date and they are superb. Ask what happened last week and they will still answer, in the same confident voice, because they do not know they are in a room. And they have never seen your company's accounts at all — those were never in any newspaper.
Lab · which zone is this claim in? pick a question a client might ask
How to fact-check, step by step
  • Sort the claim first. Is it a timeless idea, a current fact, or something private to your client? You cannot choose a check until you know which of the three you are holding.
  • Treat anything with a date or a decimal point as unverified. Market sizes, growth rates, tax rates, prices, headcounts. Precision is a writing style the model can produce; it is not evidence.
  • Ask what would have changed since. Leadership, ownership, regulation, pricing, product names. If the honest answer is "this changes every year", the model's version is out of date by construction.
  • Use a tool that shows its sources — then open them. A model with search gives you links. Links you have not opened are not sources.
  • For anything about the client, stop asking and start attaching. No prompt reaches a document you did not provide. There is no clever wording that fixes this.
The fact-checking protocol, in order
  • 1 · Sort the claim before you check it. Timeless idea, current fact, or private fact? Only the middle one is worth a web search; the first rarely needs checking and the third can never be checked, only supplied.
  • 2 · Assume anything with a date or a decimal point is unverified. Market sizes, growth rates, regulations, prices, headcounts, "as of" statements. Precision is a style the model can produce; it is not evidence.
  • 3 · Ask what would have changed since. Leadership, ownership, regulation, pricing, market share, product names. If the answer is "this changes yearly", the model's version is stale by construction.
  • 4 · Prefer tools that show their sources. A model with search attached gives you links you can open. One without gives you the same sentence with nothing behind it.
  • 5 · Never cite what you have not opened. Session 01's rule, and the reason a consulting firm refunded part of a government fee.
  • 6 · For anything about the client, stop asking and start attaching. No amount of prompting reaches a document you did not provide.
How to say this to a client

"The model has read an enormous amount, but it stopped reading on a date, and it has never read anything of yours. So we use it where the answer is a transformation of material we give it, and we source everything else independently. That is not a limitation we are working around quietly — it is written into how we quality-check the work."

Two follow-ups worth preparing for. "Can't you just connect it to the internet?" — yes, and that fixes recency but not privacy, and it introduces whatever the internet got wrong. "Then what are we paying for?" — the judgement about which claims need checking, and the accountability for the ones that went out unchecked.

For the discussion

Answering the two questions well

Q1 · What does attention let a model do that older approaches could not?

Structure: what came before and why it failed → what attention does → a business-writing example → the consequence for the work.

  • Name both older failures, not one. Bag-of-words ignored order completely. Sequential models respected order but forgot the beginning by the time they reached the end. Attention fixes both at once — and mentioning both is what separates a good answer from a repeated headline.
  • Say what it does in one sentence. Every word can look at every other word directly and weigh how much each one matters, at any distance, in a single step.
  • Use a document example, not a toy sentence. "The aforementioned penalties shall not apply" on page 31 binding back to the penalty clause on page 4. That is a real thing you will be asked to do, and it is the capability in one image.
  • Close on the consequence. Because attention removed the sequential bottleneck, training could run in parallel — which is why models could grow, and why the tool works on a whole contract rather than a paragraph.
Q2 · How should the cut-off shape the way a consultant fact-checks?

Structure: what a cut-off is → the three zones → the protocol → what changes in your working method.

  • Start with the mechanism. Training ended on a date and froze. The model is not connected to anything unless a tool has been bolted on.
  • Then split it into three, not two. Inside the training window, after it, and never in it at all because it is private. Most people remember the first two and forget the third, which is the one that hurts on an engagement.
  • Give a rule someone can apply on Monday. Anything with a date, a decimal point or a proper noun attached to it gets sourced independently. Everything about the client gets attached rather than asked.
  • Say what does not change. A tool with web search fixes recency, not privacy, and it inherits whatever the internet got wrong. The checking step is not overhead you can automate away — it is the part you are accountable for.
Three answers that will sound thin. "It pays attention to important words" — that is the word restated, not explained; say what it can now do that it could not before. "It doesn't know recent things" — true and incomplete; the private-data zone is the one that costs money. "You should always double-check AI" — everyone says this; what makes it an answer is naming which claims, in what order, against what source.

Key takeaways & glossary

If you remember only one thing

Every step in this story is an attempt to answer one question: how do you turn meaning into numbers without losing it? Counting words lost the order. Embeddings caught the meaning of words but not of sentences. Reading in order caught the sentence but forgot the paragraph. Attention finally let every part of a text weigh every other part — and once that no longer had to happen in sequence, the models could grow until they could write your first draft.

01

Language became arithmetic

Bag-of-words made text computable by counting. Crude, fast, still in production — and it threw away word order, which for a consultant reading verbatims can invert the finding.

02

Embeddings are meaning as coordinates

Words used in similar company end up near each other. This is what makes semantic search, clustering and retrieval possible — the plumbing under every "ask our documents" tool.

03

Static embeddings could not handle context

One frozen set of numbers for "bank", "interest", "margin", "security". Meaning depends on the sentence, and word2vec never read the sentence.

04

Reading in order meant forgetting

Everything the input meant had to squeeze through one fixed summary. Long inputs degraded silently, and the sequential design capped how large models could get.

05

Attention is selective focus, at any distance

Every word looks at every other word and weighs which matter. A clause on page 31 can bind to one on page 4 in a single step. That is the answer to Q1.

06

Removing sequence unlocked scale

The Transformer's real gift was parallel training. Not just better quality — the ability to grow by four orders of magnitude in six years.

07

Two branches, two budgets

Encoder-only models understand and are small and cheap; decoder-only models write and are large and rented. Tagging 40,000 tickets is the first kind, and clients routinely buy the second.

08

Parameters are unreadable dials

Which is why you cannot open the model and fix a fact. And why recent parameter counts quoted in the trillions are estimates, not published figures.

09

The raw model is not the product

Instruction tuning, preference training, domain tuning, tools, and your prompt sit between it and you. The last two are yours, cost least, and change the output most.

10

The cut-off creates three zones

Read, not read, and never public. The tone of voice is identical across all three, which is the entire fact-checking problem in one sentence.

11

Search fixes recency, not privacy

Connecting a model to the web patches the middle zone. Your client's numbers were never on the web, so they need attaching, not searching.

12

Nothing here retires the verification habit

Bigger models are wrong less often and more persuasively. Everything from Session 01 about checking, sourcing and accountability still stands.

01
The word everyone uses loosely

What "fluency" actually means here

Fluency is not knowing more features. Plenty of people can list every menu in the app and still get mediocre output, and plenty of fluent users touch four features in total. Fluency is knowing what to hand over, how to describe it, whether to believe the answer, and what you remain responsible for.

The picture to hold on toThink of a new analyst joining your team. The question is never "have they read the software manual". It is: do they know which jobs to give away and which to keep, can they brief someone clearly, can they tell good work from work that merely looks good, and do they check before it goes out. Fluency with these tools is the same four things.
The four skills, in plain words Anthropic's AI Fluency framework, used here as the spine of the session
SkillThe question it answersWhat it looks like when someone has itWhat it looks like when they don't
DelegationShould this job go to the tool at all — and which part of it?Hands over the transcription and the first draft; keeps the judgement about what mattersEither refuses to use it for anything, or hands over the whole task including the thinking

Anthropic publishes this as the "4D framework" — Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence — inside its free AI Fluency courses. It is used here because it happens to be the cleanest available vocabulary for the thing this session is about, and because you can go and take the course for nothing.

Notice what is missing from that table. Not one of the four is about knowing a product's features. Features change every few weeks — three of the things described in this session's own source article were out of date within four months. The four skills do not change, which is why they are the thing worth building.

05
The honest version

Stronger, weaker, or simply different

Most comparisons you will read declare a winner, which is a tell that the writer has not used both on real work. On the tasks an MBA consultant actually does, the honest answer is usually "either", occasionally "this one, clearly", and sometimes "neither — you wanted a spreadsheet".

Where ChatGPT is genuinely ahead
  • Anything visual. Images and video come out of the same subscription. If your deliverable includes a mood board, a storyboard or a packaging mock-up — which for a MICA audience is often — this is not a close call.
  • Breadth of the consumer surface. Voice, image editing, a store full of pre-built assistants, scheduled tasks. More things to try, more people who already know how.
  • Ubiquity. Your client has heard of it, your intern has used it, and there is a tutorial for everything. Never underestimate what that saves in a rollout.
  • The free tier. Still the most generous door into serious use, which matters when you are the one recommending what a small client should start with.
Where Claude is genuinely ahead
  • Long, careful document work. The thing it was built around, and still where its reputation sits: reading a lot and writing something disciplined about it. The test to run: hand both tools two hundred pages of supplier contracts and ask for unusual clauses with the page reference. Count the false alarms and the misses.
  • Living inside the tools you already use. The spreadsheet, the deck, Slack, the browser. Less "go to the AI", more "the AI is here".
  • Writing that needs less editing. A matter of taste rather than measurement — but it is the reason many writing-heavy teams switched, and worth testing on your own material.
  • The ad-free commitment. A stated position that conversations will not be monetised, which is an easier thing to take to a risk committee.
And a large middle where they are simply different
  • Tone. One tends to sound eager and expansive, the other more measured and willing to say a thing is uncertain. Neither is correct. Which one you want depends on whether you are drafting a pitch or a risk register — and this is the single most common reason people prefer one, even when they explain it as a capability difference.
  • Where the effort has gone. One built a bigger consumer surface, the other went deeper into enterprise workflow. Both are now converging on the same thing: an agent that produces finished files.
  • How you package a repeated job. A custom assistant in a store, or a reusable skill and project. Same idea, different plumbing.
  • The safety story. A published constitution versus usage policies and guardrails. This matters enormously in a procurement conversation and almost not at all in your Tuesday afternoon.
The consulting answer to "which should we buy?" Run a bake-off. Take twenty real tasks from the team's actual week, run them through both for a fortnight, and count edits rather than opinions. It costs two subscriptions and half a day, and it beats every comparison article ever written — including this one.

06
Discussion question 2

When to use a chat model — and when to use the boring tool instead

A chat model is not a general-purpose replacement for the things on your machine. It is very good at one specific shape of job — turning messy language into something structured — and noticeably worse than a fifty-year-old tool at several others. Reaching for it by reflex is a fluency failure just as much as refusing to use it.

The picture to hold on toA chat model is a brilliant graduate who reads fast, writes well and is bad at mental arithmetic. A spreadsheet is a calculator that never gets tired and shows its working. A search engine is a librarian who knows what was published this morning. You would not ask any one of them to do all three jobs.

A ladder you can place yourself on

Five levels of fluency

Fluency is not a badge you either have or lack. It is four separate skills that grow at different rates — and most people are much further along on one than on the others. The point of a ladder is to find the rung you are actually standing on.

A skill worth more than the comparison

How to read a tool comparison without being misled

This session was built partly from a comparison article that was updated in February 2026 — six months ago — and several of its claims were already wrong by the time you read them. That is not sloppiness on the writer's part. It is what happens to any comparison in a field that ships monthly, and learning to spot it is more useful than any single fact in this session.

Lab · is this claim still true? six claims from published comparisons
Five questions to ask of any comparison you are handed
  • When was it last updated, and what has shipped since? Anything older than a quarter is a historical document in this field. Check the date before the content.
  • Does it name specific versions, or just brands? "ChatGPT can't do X" is meaningless without a model and a plan attached, because the answer differs between free and paid on the same day.
  • Who benefits if you believe it? Vendor pages, affiliate comparison sites and consultancies selling implementation all have a position. So does this page — see the disclosure in module 03.
  • Is the claimed difference a capability or a preference? "Writes better" is taste; "generates video" is a fact. Both are useful, but only one is checkable.
  • Could you test it yourself in ten minutes? Almost always yes, on your own material — and that beats reading anybody's table, including the one in module 04.

After this session

A consultant's path for teaching yourself these tools

Nobody becomes fluent in a 75-minute session. Fluency comes from about twenty hours of deliberate use on work you actually care about — and the order matters, because the habits you build in week one are the ones you keep. Here is a path that fits alongside a term of coursework, with a concrete exercise at each stage.

The picture to hold on toLearning to drive. Nobody learns by reading the manual; nobody learns safely by being handed the keys to a motorway either. You start in an empty car park with something low-stakes, then add traffic, then add weather. Each stage here adds one kind of difficulty, and each has an exercise you could do this evening.
Lab · the six-stage path click a stage for the exercise
Three rules that make the difference between twenty hours and twenty wasted hours
  • Practise on work you have to do anyway. Not toy prompts. Your actual assignment, your actual reading, your actual summer-internship deliverable. Practice on real material is the only kind where you can tell whether the output was any good — and where you will notice the invented citation, because you know the source.
  • Keep a two-column note. One column for briefs that worked, one for outputs that were confidently wrong. Ten entries in each and you have a personal manual worth more than any course, including this one. The second column is the more valuable of the two and the one everyone skips.
  • Rebuild something you have already done by hand. The most instructive exercise there is: take a piece of work you completed last term and redo it with the tool. You know the right answer, so you can see exactly where it helped, where it drifted, and where it produced something plausible that you would have believed if you had not already known better.
Which tool for which consulting job the framework, mapped to real deliverables

Four questions decide almost every case. Work down them in order — the first two are about whether a chat model belongs in the job at all, and only the last two are about which product to open.

  • Must the answer be exact, reproducible and auditable? Then the spreadsheet, the query or the source document leads, and the model explains, drafts and checks around it. A growth rate goes in a cell; the story about the growth rate goes in a chat window.
  • Does it depend on private or current facts? Private means attach it or connect it — there is no prompt that reaches your client's accounts. Current means start at the source and cite the source, using search to find it faster.
  • Is the output a file, or a conversation? If you need a finished spreadsheet, deck or document, you want the agent surfaces — the work products, not the chat. If you need thinking, arguing or drafting, plain chat is faster and cheaper.
  • Only now: which of the two? Visual output settles it one way. Work that lives inside a spreadsheet or a deck settles it the other. Everything else is taste, decided by running the same brief in both.

For the discussion

Answering the two questions well

Q1 · Novice versus fluent, with your own examples

Structure: what fluency actually is → two or three visible differences → your prompt that worked → your prompt that failed → what you changed.

  • Do not define fluency as knowing features. Say it is four things: what you hand over, how you describe it, whether you can judge the answer, and what you stay responsible for.
  • Pick differences you can demonstrate. The strongest is attachment — novices ask it to know things, fluent users hand it the material. You can show that in ten seconds.
  • Bring the real failed prompt. Word for word, including how short it was. Then say what was missing: no material, no audience, no format, no constraint on inventing.
  • End on the second prompt. The single clearest marker of fluency is that there is always a second message, and it names what was wrong with the first answer.
Q2 · Chat model versus the ordinary tool

Structure: the shape of job it is good at → the two tests that send you elsewhere → examples both ways → the failure mode of getting it wrong in each direction.

  • Name the home ground first. Turning messy language into structure, and producing many options rather than one answer. Nothing else on your machine does either well.
  • Give the two disqualifiers. If the number must tie out and be auditable, use the spreadsheet. If it depends on what is true today, start at the source.
  • Say both failure modes. Over-use produces confident wrong numbers in a client deck. Under-use means a junior spends three days coding transcripts by hand. The second is less dramatic and more common.
  • Land on the hybrid. The mature answer is rarely one tool: the model explains and drafts, the spreadsheet computes, the source is cited. Saying that out loud is what separates a considered answer from a slogan.
Three answers that will sound thin. "Claude is better for writing, ChatGPT for everything else" — a taste claim dressed as analysis, and you will be asked how you tested it. "Fluent users write better prompts" — true and circular; say what is in the better prompt. "Always double-check the output" — everyone says this; name which claims, in what order, against what source.

Landing

Key takeaways & glossary

If you remember only one thing

The difference between a novice and a fluent user is larger than the difference between the two tools. Both are extremely capable and both will be different by next term — the model lineups on both sides turned over twice in the quarter before this session. What you carry away is not a feature list. It is knowing what to hand over, how to brief it, whether to believe the answer, and what remains yours — plus a path for building that over the next twenty hours on work you have to do anyway.

01

Fluency is four skills, not a feature list

Delegation, description, discernment, diligence. None of them is in a manual, and none of them goes out of date when the product ships a new version.

02

Both boxes are bigger than the chat window

One family reaches you through a consumer app that keeps absorbing features; the other through the spreadsheet, deck and browser you already have open.

03

The 2026 competition is agents, not chat

Both now sell something that takes an outcome and returns finished files. That is where the real comparison sits, not in who writes a nicer paragraph.

04

Real differences are few; preferences are many

Visual generation and the ad-free position are facts. Tone, and how much editing the output needs, are taste — and taste is why most people actually choose.

05

Comparison articles go stale in a quarter

The source article for this session claimed one of them had no internet access. Check the date before the content, and prefer primary documentation.

06

A bake-off beats every comparison

Twenty real tasks, two subscriptions, a fortnight, count the edits. Half a day of work that settles an argument no article can.

07

The first two questions send you away from the chat

Must it be exact and auditable? Does it depend on today? If yes, the spreadsheet and the source come first, and the model explains afterwards.

08

Under-use is the commoner failure

Over-use produces a wrong number in a deck and everyone notices. Under-use quietly costs a junior three days of hand-coding transcripts and nobody counts it.

09

Novices ask it to know; fluent users hand it the material

The single most visible difference, and the easiest habit to fix today. Attach the document before you type the question.

10

There is always a second message

The fluent user's second prompt names what was wrong with the first answer. If your conversations are one message long, that is the thing to change this week.

11

Twenty hours, in the right order

Paste something in, write one real brief, break it on purpose, redo something you have already done, connect your material, package what you repeat. Each stage assumes the habit before it.

12

The framework is four questions, and the first two are not about the brand

Must it be exact and auditable? Does it depend on private or current facts? Is the output a file or a conversation? Only then: which of the two products.

13

Free courses, yes; proctored exams, probably not

Two free fluency courses will teach you both products properly. The vendor exams are aimed at partner delivery teams and engineers, not at this room.

14

A portfolio beats a badge

Three artefacts you actually made — a coded transcript set, a rebuilt deck, a working model — will move a recruiter further than any certificate on the page.

ACTIVITY 01

Delegate or keep?

Why you are doing this. The first of the four fluency skills is knowing which parts of a job to hand over. Two opposite mistakes are equally common: handing over the whole thing including the judgement, and refusing to hand over anything at all. Both look like caution and both cost you.

What to do. Five situations from real engagements. For each, decide what you would give the tool and what you would keep, click your answer, and read why. Watch for the pattern: the tool gets the reading and the drafting, you keep the decision and the check.

Five questions · about five minutes · nothing to prepare
Score: 0 / 5

The pattern underneath these five

Hand over the work that is reading, restructuring or generating options. Keep the work that carries your name: the recommendation, the verification and anything that depends on knowing the client. Nothing on that list changes when the products update.

ACTIVITY 02

Fix the brief

Why you are doing this. This is direct preparation for the first discussion question. Most disappointing output is a briefing failure, not a model failure — the same tool, given a proper brief, produces something you edit for ten minutes instead of rewriting. The aim is that you can name exactly what a weak prompt is missing.

What to do. Five weak prompts. For each, decide which single change would improve it most before you click. Then read the explanation, which says what the fix actually buys you.

Stuck? Open the prompt post-mortems in the labs tab and read a pair or two first
Score: 0 / 5

Then do the part that is actually yours

The discussion question asks for one prompt of yours that worked and one that failed. Go and find the failure in your own history — the real words, not a tidied-up version — and write down what was missing: the material, the reader, the format, or a constraint on inventing. That sentence is your answer to Q1.

ACTIVITY 03

Which tool?

Why you are doing this. Two decisions get muddled in practice: ChatGPT or Claude, and chat model or ordinary tool. The second matters far more. This activity mixes both so you have to notice which question you are actually being asked.

What to do. Five tasks. For each, decide whether it is a genuine ChatGPT-or-Claude question, a case where either works, or a case where you should not be in a chat window at all.

Notice how often the honest answer is “either” — that is a finding, not a dodge
Score: 0 / 5

What to carry away

There are only a handful of true tie-breakers between the two products: visual generation, where the work lives, and how sensitive the conversations are. Everything else is taste, settled by running the same brief in both on your own material. But the chat-or-spreadsheet question has a right answer, and getting it wrong is what puts a made-up number in a client deck.

ACTIVITY 04

Place the user

Why you are doing this. Fluency is four separate skills that grow at different rates, and your weakest one sets your ceiling. Learning to spot which skill is missing — in a colleague, in a client's team, in yourself — is what lets you fix the right thing instead of buying more training on prompting.

What to do. Five descriptions of real behaviour. For each, decide which of the four skills is weak, or which rung of the ladder the person is standing on.

Then open the fluency ladder in the labs tab and place yourself honestly
Score: 0 / 5

The uncomfortable pattern

Most people in a business school room score high on description and low on discernment, because prompting is what gets taught and checking is what gets skipped. That combination — a beautifully briefed, entirely unverified answer — is the most dangerous point on the whole ladder, because the output looks more credible rather than less.

ACTIVITY 05

Spot the stale claim

Why you are doing this. Every comparison of these tools starts going out of date the day it is published, including the one in these notes. Being the person in the room who checks a claim before repeating it is worth more than knowing any particular fact, and it takes thirty seconds.

What to do. Five situations involving published claims. Decide what you would actually do in each, then read why. The habit being built is: check the date, check the model and plan, check the primary page.

Try it live: pick one claim from the notes and see whether the vendor's own page still supports it
Score: 0 / 5

The five questions to ask of any comparison

When was it updated and what has shipped since? Does it name models and plans, or just brands? Who benefits if you believe it? Is the difference a capability or a preference? And could you test it yourself in ten minutes — because the answer is almost always yes, on your own material.

ACTIVITY 06

Client objections

Why you are doing this. Knowing which tool suits which job and being able to defend that view to a client who has already made up their mind are different skills, and only the second one gets you hired. These are six things clients genuinely say in the first meeting.

What to do. Read each objection. Say your answer out loud, in your own words, before you open the card — actually out loud, because that is where you discover whether you know it. Then open it and see what you left out.

No scores · the measure is whether your answer would have satisfied the person who asked

ACTIVITY 08

Plan your own twenty hours

Why you are doing this. Nobody becomes fluent in a 75-minute session. It takes roughly twenty hours of deliberate use on work you actually care about — and you have a Claude Pro subscription for this course, so the only missing ingredient is a plan. This activity turns the six-stage path into something with dates on it.

What to do. Open the six-stage path in the labs tab and read the exercise attached to each stage. Then answer the five questions below to work out where you actually are and what the next fortnight should look like.

The exercises are designed to run on your own coursework, not on toy problems
Score: 0 / 5

Where this goes next in the course

Everything in this session is the manual version — you, driving the tool one task at a time. From Module 4: Agents in Consulting Practice the same deliverables get done properly with agents: context management and synthesis, market and competitive research at scale, financial models and quant work, data science and analytics, finding real insight, and C-suite outputs, then critique and iteration. Module 5 is the end-to-end capstone. The more of these twenty hours you do now, the more of Module 4 is about design rather than about finding the buttons.

Debrief — five questions for yourself
  • Which of the four skills is your weakest? Not the one you enjoy — the one you skip. For most people it is discernment, and the fix is a checking routine rather than a better prompt.
  • What did your failed prompt actually leave out? Material, reader, format, or a constraint on inventing. Name it in one sentence; that sentence is your answer to the first discussion question.
  • When did you last use a chat model for something a spreadsheet does better? And when did you last hand-code something for three days that could have taken twenty minutes?
  • Which claim in these notes would you check before repeating? Several will be out of date within the term. Knowing which ones are volatile is itself the skill.
  • One habit to take away. Attach the material before you type the question, or always send a second message. Pick one and use it this week.
